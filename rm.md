Abi has an ethical and professional obligation to report the complete and accurate findings, including both positive and negative results. Presenting only selective analyses constitutes data bias and misrepresentation, which can distort public understanding and harm consumers. The BCS Code of Conduct (2021) similarly emphasizes acting in the public interest and ensuring that outputs are not used to deceive or cause harm.

Legally, if the company were to publish misleading nutritional claims, it could breach consumer protection laws such as the UK Consumer Protection from Unfair Trading Regulations (2008) or the U.S. Federal Trade Commission Act (1938). Socially, such misconduct could erode public trust in both scientific research and the food industry.

Abi’s most ethical course of action is to submit a truthful report and formally document all analyses, ensuring transparency and reproducibility. If the manufacturer intends to misrepresent the data, Abi should escalate the concern to his institution’s ethics board or consider whistleblowing through appropriate legal channels.

### Unit 3 Methodology and Research Methods <a name="unit3"></a>

This unit explored the philosophical and practical foundations of research design. It introduced the distinction between exploratory and descriptive designs and compared quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods approaches. The key takeaway was that every methodological choice reflects the researcher’s underlying assumptions about reality (ontology), knowledge (epistemology), and values (axiology). Understanding these positions is critical when aligning research questions, data-collection strategies, and analytical techniques.

**Activity: Research Proposal Review**

1. Suitable Research Method

Given the emerging and ethically complex nature of this topic, a qualitative exploratory design is the most appropriate. The research aims to understand how and why deep learning applications in suspect profiling raise specific ethical and methodological issues, rather than to measure their statistical prevalence.

A qualitative approach allows in-depth exploration of perspectives from existing case studies, scholarly analyses, and legal frameworks. Within this paradigm, the study will use secondary research through document and literature analysis. However, an element of mixed methods may later be incorporated by comparing quantitative performance data (e.g., accuracy rates or bias metrics) from existing models to contextualize ethical discussions.

This choice aligns with Saunders et al. (2023), who emphasize matching design to research purpose and assumptions: here, an interpretivist stance acknowledges that understanding ethical implications requires subjective interpretation of human values, not only empirical validation.

2. Data Collection Methods

For this project, the main data sources will be secondary qualitative materials:

Academic and technical publications on deep learning, facial recognition, and NLP in law enforcement.

Policy documents and legal texts such as GDPR, the EU AI Act, and national policing frameworks.

Ethical codes and professional standards (ACM, BCS) to provide normative benchmarks.

Case studies and audit reports of systems such as PredPol or COMPAS to illustrate real-world implications.

Data will be collected through structured database searches (Scopus, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar) and organized thematically. Should quantitative data be integrated, these will consist of published model-performance statistics or fairness metrics from open reports rather than original experiments.

3. Required and Developing Skills

To conduct this study effectively, several skills are essential:

a. Literature Search and Critical Evaluation

The ability to identify, compare, and critically appraise interdisciplinary sources across AI, criminology, and ethics is central. I will continue strengthening my database-search and citation-management skills.

b. Thematic Analysis and Synthesis

Competence in qualitative coding and pattern recognition is required to extract and interpret recurring ethical and methodological themes from the literature.

c. Understanding of AI and ML Concepts

A technical grasp of deep learning architectures (CNNs, RNNs, transformers) is necessary to interpret methodological discussions accurately and avoid misrepresenting performance or limitations.

d. Legal and Ethical Literacy

Familiarity with data protection laws, human-rights principles, and algorithmic governance will help ground ethical arguments in concrete policy contexts.

e. Academic Writing and Research Communication

I will continue refining my academic writing to express nuanced arguments clearly, integrating cross-disciplinary terminology while maintaining coherence and proper referencing.

4. Reflection

This activity clarified how methodological choice reflects not only technical feasibility but also personal research philosophy. I identified strongly with an interpretivist perspective because I am less interested in measuring accuracy numerically and more focused on how technological systems intersect with human rights and ethics.

Exploring qualitative and exploratory designs also made me realize that flexibility is a strength: complex topics like AI ethics benefit from iterative inquiry rather than rigid hypotheses. At the same time, reviewing mixed-method possibilities encouraged me to consider how limited quantitative comparisons could add context without compromising the reflective nature of the study.

I now better appreciate that methodology is not merely procedural; it shapes how knowledge is created and interpreted. As I develop my full proposal, I will deepen my understanding of research ethics approval processes and qualitative analysis frameworks such as Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis, ensuring that my work remains both ambitious and ethically grounded.

### Unit 5 Interviews, Survey Methods, and Questionnaire Design  <a name="unit5"></a>

Unit 5 dives into interviews, surveys, and pre-/post-testing methods in research. It examined how structured, semi-structured, and unstructured interviews gather deep insight into individual experiences, while surveys enable large-scale data collection and trend analysis. The unit also emphasized the ethical and methodological importance of designing valid, transparent, and consent-based questionnaires. It highlighted how misused survey methods can result in serious privacy violations, data exploitation, and reputational damage for researchers and organizations.

**Activity: Case Study – Inappropriate Use of Surveys**

In 2018, the Cambridge Analytica scandal exposed how data from approximately 87 million Facebook users was harvested without informed consent (Confessore, 2018). The company acquired this data via an innocuous “personality quiz” app developed by researcher Aleksandr Kogan under the guise of academic study. Users consented to share their personal information, but the app’s design also collected data from their entire friend networks, violating both ethical standards and Facebook’s platform policies.

Cambridge Analytica then used this information to build detailed psychographic profiles that targeted individuals with political advertising during the 2016 U.S. presidential election and the Brexit campaign. The case illustrates how a seemingly harmless survey can be weaponized to manipulate public opinion, undermining democratic integrity and user privacy.

**How it happened:**
The survey was presented as an academic personality test but included permissions that granted broad access to user networks. The data were exported and repurposed for political micro-targeting without informed consent or transparency about intent.

**Why it was used:**
The firm exploited public trust in social-media quizzes to collect large-scale behavioral data cheaply and discreetly. By mapping psychological traits and voting tendencies, Cambridge Analytica sought to design persuasive, individualized political messages to influence voter behavior.

Ethical, Social, Legal, and Professional Impact:

Ethical: Violated principles of informed consent, autonomy, and beneficence. Participants were deceived about data purpose and scope.

Social: Deepened distrust in technology companies and highlighted the manipulative potential of behavioral analytics in democratic processes.

Legal: Led to multiple investigations under GDPR and the US Federal Trade Commission, resulting in Facebook’s $5 billion fine in 2019.

Professional: Contravened codes such as ACM 1.2 (Avoid harm) and BCS 1(a) (Have due regard for privacy and wellbeing) by prioritizing profit and political gain over public interest.

**Secondary Example: Quiz Apps and Data Harvesting for Marketing**

A similar misuse occurred in 2020 when several “fun personality quizzes” on social media were linked to third-party marketing firms that collected demographic and behavioral data to sell to advertisers (BBC News, 2020). While less politically charged, these surveys also failed to provide informed consent and lacked clear opt-out options.

**Impact:**

Ethical: Users’ autonomy and privacy were again compromised.

Social: Normalized data harvesting as entertainment, blurring the boundary between consent and manipulation.

Legal: Many of these quizzes operated outside GDPR jurisdiction, exploiting international data-transfer loopholes.

Professional: Undermined trust in legitimate academic or commercial research using online questionnaires.

## Unit 6 Quantitative Methods: Descriptive and Inferential Statistics <a name="unit6"></a>

This unit introduced the foundations of quantitative research, focusing on how numerical data can be analyzed to reveal trends, relationships, and patterns. The discussion centered on descriptive statistics — such as measures of location (mean, median, mode) and dispersion (range, variance, standard deviation) — as well as the conceptual bridge to inferential statistics, which enables generalization from samples to populations.
Understanding how to summarize and interpret quantitative data is essential for producing objective, reproducible findings and ensuring that analytical conclusions align with the research question.

This unit helped me see how quantitative and qualitative approaches complement rather than oppose each other. In my field, ethical evaluation of deep learning must rest on both narrative interpretation and empirical verification. Understanding measures of central tendency and variability gave me a clearer sense of how data can expose inequality or bias in algorithmic systems.

Going forward, I will integrate basic statistical measures into my research pipeline, not to quantify morality but to substantiate ethical arguments with evidence. This balance between numerical precision and human-centered interpretation defines what it means to be a responsible data scientist.

## Unit 12 Project Management and Managing Risk <a name="unit12"></a>

Project management in computing involves resources, coordinating, and people to deliver an outcome. Often under constraints of time, budget, and scope. In data science, the effectiveness of project management ensures that every step aligns with both ethical and business standards. The module emphasis has been on that there is no universal method that fits all projects. Instead, everything requires an adaptive approach and considerations. I've learned in frameworks such as Agile, Scrum, and PRINCE2, are all effective depending on the environment. 

Project life cycles typically include:
- Initiation
- Planning
- Execution
- Monitoring and Control
- Closure

In Agile iterative models align well with experimentation cycles. Each "sprint" represents a research milestone, however, maintaining documentation and audit trails remains essential, especially in ethical and regulated contexts. 

A core insight from this unit is that no project is risk-free. From technical to ethical, managing uncertainty means knowing what is unknown while preparing for contingencies. In the context of my research, ethical risk management is paramount. Potential risks include biased training data or misinterpretation of outputs. Mitigation strategies include dataset audits, explainability methods, and stakeholder reviews before deployment.
