**Initial Post:**

The Blocker Plus case illustrates the ethical tensions that arise when machine learning systems are deployed in socially sensitive contexts such as education. While the system aimed to comply with the U.S. Children’s Internet Protection Act (CIPA), the inclusion of biased user feedback led to discriminatory censorship of legitimate content. By failing to safeguard against malicious input, Blocker Plus violated these principles, resulting in social harm and the suppression of protected speech.

From the perspective of the BCS Code of Conduct, Section 1(a) requires members to “have due regard for public health, privacy, security and wellbeing.” Furthermore, Section 2(c) emphasizes the need to “avoid situations that give rise to conflicts of interest or cause unfair bias.” The developers’ decision to retain the corrupted model for business reasons conflicts with these professional duties.

Legally, although CIPA compliance was maintained, the discriminatory blocking of LGBTQ+ content may contravene equality and free speech protections, depending on jurisdiction. Socially, the case demonstrates the risk of embedding algorithmic bias into critical infrastructure, underscoring the ethical responsibility for transparency, accountability, and robust system design.

### Unit 1 The Scientific Investigation and Ethics in Computing <a name="unit1"></a>

This unit focused on the types of reasoning used in the scientific method of problem-solving. The two core types are deductive and inductive reasoning. 

For our unit 1 activity, we were tasked with writing a reflective activity titled **Ethics in Computing in the age of Generative AI**.

(Rolfe et al., 2001 framework: What? So What? Now What?)

**What?**

The rapid rise of generative AI since late 2022 has reshaped almost every field of activity, including computer science itself. Systems such as ChatGPT, DALL·E, and Copilot have made artificial intelligence mainstream, but they have also exposed deep ethical and governance challenges around privacy, intellectual property, disinformation, and accountability.

In their paper, Correa et al. (2023) emphasize that while global stakeholders agree on the importance of ethical AI, there is no international consensus on the values that should guide AI development. They highlight that the diversity of legal systems, cultural contexts, and policy priorities makes it difficult to harmonize standards, yet the need for shared governance tools is increasingly urgent. Similarly, Deckard (2023) warns that without coordinated oversight, AI may advance faster than society’s ability to manage its consequences, especially in areas such as surveillance, data protection, and misinformation.

As a computing professional entering the field, I find this debate both exciting and concerning. I am inspired by the creative potential of AI but equally aware of the ethical risks of unchecked innovation. Reflecting on these readings, I recognize that regulation is not an obstacle to progress but a foundation for sustainable and trustworthy AI development.

**So What?**

From a professional and ethical perspective, the absence of unified AI governance threatens several key principles of computing ethics. Both the ACM (2018) and BCS (2021) Codes of Conduct stress that professionals must avoid harm, act in the public interest, and ensure that their products are used responsibly. When organizations prioritize rapid deployment over ethical evaluation, they risk violating these responsibilities.

The fragmented state of AI policy across nations adds to this problem. For example, the EU AI Act uses a risk-based regulatory approach that classifies AI systems by potential harm, while the United States relies more on voluntary frameworks such as the NIST AI Risk Management Framework. Meanwhile, countries like China are introducing stricter content and model approval regulations driven by national security and social stability priorities. As Correa et al. (2023) note, this divergence creates uncertainty for global companies and inconsistent protections for users.

Reflecting on these differences, I see how they mirror larger philosophical divides. Should AI ethics be guided by universal human rights principles or by context-specific cultural norms? Personally, I believe a hybrid model is necessary: one that enshrines universal protections for privacy, non-discrimination, and transparency while allowing flexibility for local interpretation.

Professionally, I also recognize the implications for developers like myself. As Deckard (2023) observes, the growing complexity of AI systems blurs lines of accountability. Engineers may not fully understand how models generate outputs, yet they remain responsible for potential harms. This realization underscores the importance of continuous ethical reflection and interdisciplinary collaboration. Legal compliance alone is insufficient; as a professional, I must cultivate an ethical mindset that questions should we build this?, not only can we build this?

Socially, the risks of unregulated AI are already visible in the spread of deepfakes, automated disinformation, and biased algorithms. These outcomes erode public trust and disproportionately harm marginalized communities. If left unchecked, they could worsen inequality and undermine democratic institutions. Therefore, effective regulation is not merely a technical matter; it is a societal necessity to ensure fairness, accountability, and respect for human dignity.

**Now What?**

Moving forward, I believe computing professionals must play an active role in shaping AI regulation rather than waiting for policymakers to dictate the rules. Developers and data scientists have insider knowledge of how algorithms operate, and their participation is essential to creating realistic, enforceable governance frameworks. I see this as part of my professional duty to contribute to the ethical evolution of my field.

Practically, this means embedding ethical review processes throughout the AI lifecycle. For example, implementing bias audits, explainability testing, and impact assessments should become standard practice before system deployment. These align with the OECD AI Principles (2019), which advocate for transparency, accountability, and robustness as global best practices. I also intend to keep my technical work aligned with privacy-by-design and fairness-by-design approaches, ensuring that user protection is integral rather than reactive.

From a legal standpoint, I support a model similar to the EU AI Act, but with stronger global cooperation. A shared international registry of high-risk AI systems, as suggested by Correa et al. (2023), could promote transparency and collective accountability. Industry consortia, such as the Partnership on AI, already provide collaborative spaces for this, but they require more binding commitments and governmental backing.

Personally, this reflection has altered my perspective on innovation. I once saw regulation as a limitation; now I see it as a mechanism that preserves public trust and long-term innovation potential. Over-regulation may slow early development, but under-regulation risks far greater harm by eroding user confidence, inviting misuse, and damaging the credibility of computing professionals.

In my career, I will promote ethical awareness within any team or organization I work with. This includes promoting transparency in algorithmic decision-making, supporting the use of open and explainable models, and encouraging diverse stakeholder input in AI design. I will also stay informed on emerging governance literature and participate in continuous professional development related to AI ethics. This reflection has reinforced my belief that computing is not just a technical discipline but also a moral and social one. The responsibility of ensuring that technology serves humanity equitably must be shared by all professionals. Regulation, when thoughtfully designed and globally coordinated, is not an adversary of innovation but its ethical compass.

### Unit 2 Research Questions, the Literature Review and the Research Proposal <a name="unit2"></a>

This unit focused on understanding the structure and purpose of a research proposal, beginning with the formulation of a clear and viable research question. The process involved defining a topic of interest, identifying research gaps through a literature review, and outlining a coherent proposal that demonstrates methodological and ethical awareness. The unit also emphasized the creative and critical aspects of refining a research question into a focused, researchable form.

Activity: Literature Review and Research Proposal Outline.

Chosen Topic: To what extent can deep learning enhance suspect profiling, and what ethical and methodological challenges limit its application?

Aim:

To critically evaluate the contribution of deep learning to suspect profiling and to identify the ethical, methodological, and legal constraints that limit its responsible application.

Objectives:

Examine the evolution of suspect profiling and its shift from behavioural to computational methods.

Assess the performance and limitations of deep learning approaches (vision-based, NLP, and hybrid).

Analyse ethical and legal challenges including bias, privacy, explainability, and due process.

Identify research gaps and propose principles for fairness-by-design and accountable AI in forensic contexts.

Proposal Outline

1. Introduction and Background:
   
Positions suspect profiling within the history of criminology and explains the transformative yet controversial influence of deep learning. Introduces the tension between technological potential and ethical constraint.

2. Literature Review:
 
Synthesises current findings on deep learning for visual, textual, and hybrid profiling. Critically discusses reproducibility, dataset bias, and legal implications such as GDPR and the EU AI Act.

3. Methodology:

Employs a qualitative meta-analysis of published studies, supported by thematic content analysis to categorise ethical and methodological challenges. Data will be drawn from peer-reviewed journals, technical reports, and legal documents.

4. Ethical Considerations:

Reviews data-protection laws and professional codes (ACM 2018; BCS 2021). Addresses informed consent, bias mitigation, and algorithmic transparency. Explores fairness-by-design as a methodological safeguard.

5. Expected Outcomes:

Produce a conceptual framework outlining the boundary conditions for ethical DL use in policing. Aims to contribute practical recommendations for governance, auditing, and interdisciplinary collaboration.

6. Limitations:
 
Acknowledges potential publication bias in available literature and the difficulty of accessing proprietary law-enforcement datasets. Notes the evolving nature of legal regulation.

Reflection

This unit deepened my understanding of how a literature review evolves into a structured proposal. Working through my deep learning in suspect profiling topic clarified the need to balance technological enthusiasm with ethical vigilance. The most challenging aspect was narrowing the question to something researchable without oversimplifying the complexity of AI ethics. I realized that refining a question is an iterative process shaped by evidence rather than opinion.

Conducting the literature review also taught me the importance of evaluating methodology, not just findings. Many studies report high model accuracy yet fail to address dataset bias or transparency. This observation influenced my research design by emphasizing interpretability and fairness over pure performance. Moving forward, I intend to explore frameworks for explainable AI and legal accountability to strengthen both the ethical and methodological credibility of my project.

### Unit 3 Methodology and Research Methods <a name="unit3"></a>

This unit explored the philosophical and practical foundations of research design. It introduced the distinction between exploratory and descriptive designs and compared quantitative, qualitative, and mixed-methods approaches. The key takeaway was that every methodological choice reflects the researcher’s underlying assumptions about reality (ontology), knowledge (epistemology), and values (axiology). Understanding these positions is critical when aligning research questions, data-collection strategies, and analytical techniques.

**Activity: Research Proposal Review**

1. Suitable Research Method

Given the emerging and ethically complex nature of this topic, a qualitative exploratory design is the most appropriate. The research aims to understand how and why deep learning applications in suspect profiling raise specific ethical and methodological issues, rather than to measure their statistical prevalence.

A qualitative approach allows in-depth exploration of perspectives from existing case studies, scholarly analyses, and legal frameworks. Within this paradigm, the study will use secondary research through document and literature analysis. However, an element of mixed methods may later be incorporated by comparing quantitative performance data (e.g., accuracy rates or bias metrics) from existing models to contextualize ethical discussions.

This choice aligns with Saunders et al. (2023), who emphasize matching design to research purpose and assumptions: here, an interpretivist stance acknowledges that understanding ethical implications requires subjective interpretation of human values, not only empirical validation.

2. Data Collection Methods

For this project, the main data sources will be secondary qualitative materials:

Academic and technical publications on deep learning, facial recognition, and NLP in law enforcement.

Policy documents and legal texts such as GDPR, the EU AI Act, and national policing frameworks.

Ethical codes and professional standards (ACM, BCS) to provide normative benchmarks.

Case studies and audit reports of systems such as PredPol or COMPAS to illustrate real-world implications.

Data will be collected through structured database searches (Scopus, IEEE Xplore, Google Scholar) and organized thematically. Should quantitative data be integrated, these will consist of published model-performance statistics or fairness metrics from open reports rather than original experiments.

3. Required and Developing Skills

To conduct this study effectively, several skills are essential:

a. Literature Search and Critical Evaluation

The ability to identify, compare, and critically appraise interdisciplinary sources across AI, criminology, and ethics is central. I will continue strengthening my database-search and citation-management skills.

b. Thematic Analysis and Synthesis

Competence in qualitative coding and pattern recognition is required to extract and interpret recurring ethical and methodological themes from the literature.

c. Understanding of AI and ML Concepts

A technical grasp of deep learning architectures (CNNs, RNNs, transformers) is necessary to interpret methodological discussions accurately and avoid misrepresenting performance or limitations.

d. Legal and Ethical Literacy

Familiarity with data protection laws, human-rights principles, and algorithmic governance will help ground ethical arguments in concrete policy contexts.

e. Academic Writing and Research Communication

I will continue refining my academic writing to express nuanced arguments clearly, integrating cross-disciplinary terminology while maintaining coherence and proper referencing.

4. Reflection

This activity clarified how methodological choice reflects not only technical feasibility but also personal research philosophy. I identified strongly with an interpretivist perspective because I am less interested in measuring accuracy numerically and more focused on how technological systems intersect with human rights and ethics.

Exploring qualitative and exploratory designs also made me realize that flexibility is a strength: complex topics like AI ethics benefit from iterative inquiry rather than rigid hypotheses. At the same time, reviewing mixed-method possibilities encouraged me to consider how limited quantitative comparisons could add context without compromising the reflective nature of the study.

I now better appreciate that methodology is not merely procedural; it shapes how knowledge is created and interpreted. As I develop my full proposal, I will deepen my understanding of research ethics approval processes and qualitative analysis frameworks such as Braun and Clarke’s thematic analysis, ensuring that my work remains both ambitious and ethically grounded.

### Unit 4: Case Studies, Focus Groups and Observations <a name="unit4"></a>

Unit 4 has no formal e-portfolio activities; instead, it introduced key qualitative data collection methods: case studies, focus groups, and observations. It examined how these methods can provide rich, contextual data about human experiences and systems. The unit also emphasized the importance of selecting data collection techniques aligned with research aims and ethical constraints, particularly when working with sensitive or high-risk topics such as law enforcement and artificial intelligence.

Exploring case studies as a primary method helped me see how contextual depth can reveal the human and systemic factors often obscured by technical metrics.

I initially considered focus groups to explore practitioner perspectives, but I realized that in the context of AI ethics and law enforcement, obtaining such data would raise significant procedural and ethical barriers. Instead, analysing documented real-world cases provides a safe, valid, and insightful approach that still enables rich interpretation.

Moving forward, I plan to explore case study methodology in greater detail, especially Yin’s (2018) and Stake’s (1995) approaches, to ensure my design aligns with established best practices in qualitative inquiry.

### Unit 5 Interviews, Survey Methods, and Questionnaire Design  <a name="unit5"></a>

Unit 5 dives into interviews, surveys, and pre-/post-testing methods in research. It examined how structured, semi-structured, and unstructured interviews gather deep insight into individual experiences, while surveys enable large-scale data collection and trend analysis. The unit also emphasized the ethical and methodological importance of designing valid, transparent, and consent-based questionnaires. It highlighted how misused survey methods can result in serious privacy violations, data exploitation, and reputational damage for researchers and organizations.

**Activity: Case Study – Inappropriate Use of Surveys**

In 2018, the Cambridge Analytica scandal exposed how data from approximately 87 million Facebook users was harvested without informed consent (Confessore, 2018). The company acquired this data via an innocuous “personality quiz” app developed by researcher Aleksandr Kogan under the guise of academic study. Users consented to share their personal information, but the app’s design also collected data from their entire friend networks, violating both ethical standards and Facebook’s platform policies.

Cambridge Analytica then used this information to build detailed psychographic profiles that targeted individuals with political advertising during the 2016 U.S. presidential election and the Brexit campaign. The case illustrates how a seemingly harmless survey can be weaponized to manipulate public opinion, undermining democratic integrity and user privacy.

**How it happened:**
The survey was presented as an academic personality test but included permissions that granted broad access to user networks. The data were exported and repurposed for political micro-targeting without informed consent or transparency about intent.

**Why it was used:**
The firm exploited public trust in social-media quizzes to collect large-scale behavioral data cheaply and discreetly. By mapping psychological traits and voting tendencies, Cambridge Analytica sought to design persuasive, individualized political messages to influence voter behavior.

Ethical, Social, Legal, and Professional Impact:

Ethical: Violated principles of informed consent, autonomy, and beneficence. Participants were deceived about data purpose and scope.

Social: Deepened distrust in technology companies and highlighted the manipulative potential of behavioral analytics in democratic processes.

Legal: Led to multiple investigations under GDPR and the US Federal Trade Commission, resulting in Facebook’s $5 billion fine in 2019.

Professional: Contravened codes such as ACM 1.2 (Avoid harm) and BCS 1(a) (Have due regard for privacy and wellbeing) by prioritizing profit and political gain over public interest.

**Secondary Example: Quiz Apps and Data Harvesting for Marketing**

A similar misuse occurred in 2020 when several “fun personality quizzes” on social media were linked to third-party marketing firms that collected demographic and behavioral data to sell to advertisers (BBC News, 2020). While less politically charged, these surveys also failed to provide informed consent and lacked clear opt-out options.

**Impact:**

Ethical: Users’ autonomy and privacy were again compromised.

Social: Normalized data harvesting as entertainment, blurring the boundary between consent and manipulation.

Legal: Many of these quizzes operated outside GDPR jurisdiction, exploiting international data-transfer loopholes.

Professional: Undermined trust in legitimate academic or commercial research using online questionnaires.

## Unit 6 Quantitative Methods: Descriptive and Inferential Statistics <a name="unit6"></a>

This unit introduced the foundations of quantitative research, focusing on how numerical data can be analyzed to reveal trends, relationships, and patterns. The discussion centered on descriptive statistics — such as measures of location (mean, median, mode) and dispersion (range, variance, standard deviation) — as well as the conceptual bridge to inferential statistics, which enables generalization from samples to populations.
Understanding how to summarize and interpret quantitative data is essential for producing objective, reproducible findings and ensuring that analytical conclusions align with the research question.

This unit helped me see how quantitative and qualitative approaches complement rather than oppose each other. In my field, ethical evaluation of deep learning must rest on both narrative interpretation and empirical verification. Understanding measures of central tendency and variability gave me a clearer sense of how data can expose inequality or bias in algorithmic systems.

Going forward, I will integrate basic statistical measures into my research pipeline, not to quantify morality but to substantiate ethical arguments with evidence. This balance between numerical precision and human-centered interpretation defines what it means to be a responsible data scientist.
